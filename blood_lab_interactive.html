What is happening?

  1. Donor red blood cells enter the recipient's circulation.
  2. Recipient antibodies may recognize matching donor antigens.
  3. Antibodies can bind to red blood cell antigens.
  4. Incompatible transfusions can cause agglutination and red blood cell destruction (hemolysis).

ABO Blood Group System

Four groups determined by A and B antigens on red blood cells.

Group A

🔵 A antigen

Anti-B antibodies

Group B

🟢 B antigen

Anti-A antibodies

Group AB

🔵 A + 🟢 B

Neither anti-A nor anti-B

Group O

No A or B antigens

Anti-A + Anti-B

Why does a reaction occur?

For example, an A-group recipient normally has anti-B antibodies. If B-antigen-bearing red blood cells enter their bloodstream, those antibodies can bind to the B antigens, potentially causing a dangerous transfusion reaction.

Rh(D) Blood Group System

A separate antigen system from ABO.

Rh-positive (+)

🟣

D antigen present

Rh-negative (−)

⚪

D antigen absent

Why isn't anti-D naturally present?

Anti-D usually develops after exposure to D-positive red blood cells, such as through transfusion or pregnancy. An Rh-negative person can become sensitized and develop immune memory.

Important: Rh-negative does not mean immune to D

It means their own red blood cells lack the D antigen. Their immune system can still recognize D as foreign following exposure.

Adaptive Immune Response

How your body develops a specific antibody response.

🔍
1. Recognition

An immune cell recognizes a foreign antigen.

⚡
2. Activation

Appropriate B cells receive activation signals.

🧬
3. Clonal expansion

The activated B cells multiply.

🏭
4. Antibody production

Plasma cells produce antigen-specific antibodies.

🧠
5. Immune memory

Memory B cells can support a faster response to future exposure.

Cybersecurity analogy

Foreign antigen = unfamiliar intruder. Immune recognition = threat detection. Antibodies = specialized defensive tools. Memory B cells = retained threat knowledge.
